2. PROBLEM AND INTENDED USERS

Students usually meet food webs through examples that start with plants and sunlight. This makes an ocean ecosystem beneath thick ice difficult to imagine. Yet chemical reactions can provide energy for microbial life even without photosynthesis. Our challenge is to communicate that possibility while distinguishing observation from speculation. We focus on one fictional hydrothermal vent field rather than claiming an entire moon is inhabited.

Our main audience is high-school students aged 14–18, with science teachers as secondary users. An interactive dive, diagram and short explanatory video help learners trace energy from rock-water chemistry through three possible levels of life. The fictional organisms are teaching models, not discoveries. We must not report any user-test results unless the team has actually conducted the test.

3. PROPOSED SOLUTION AND HOW IT WORKS

The Hydrogen Garden is a fictional field of white carbonate chimneys on the seafloor of Pyrothalasa, an icy moon inspired by Saturn's Enceladus. The habitat lies 50 km beneath the surface: 20 km of ice above 30 km of salty ocean. Our model deliberately assumes negligible dissolved oxygen; this is not proven merely by the absence of sunlight. The habitat is powered by hydrogen released when water reacts with olivine-rich rock (serpentinization). Methane-making microorganisms (methanogens) use hydrogen and biologically available inorganic carbon to obtain energy and produce biomass.

The proposed three-level food web is: (1) methane-makers, (2) fictional single-celled grazers supported by a wider microbial food community, and (3) Shadowfin, a fictional microscopic predator below 1 mm. Methanogens' growth is supported by Earth-based experiments under some Enceladus-like conditions. Feeding links and Shadowfin's anaerobic metabolism remain unverified hypotheses. The website visualizes the journey, while the PDF separates evidence from assumptions.

4. SUPPORTING SCIENCE

4a. HABITAT CONDITIONS

Depth | Seafloor 50 km below surface; 20 km ice and 30 km water | Fictional dimensions informed by Enceladus ocean models (Thomas et al., 2016; NASA).
Pressure | Approximately 56 bar at the floor | Calculated hydrostatic approximation: 920 kg/m3 × 0.113 m/s2 × 20,000 m plus 1,030 kg/m3 × 0.113 m/s2 × 30,000 m ≈ 5.57 MPa. Density and constant gravity are simplifications; surface pressure is neglected.

Light | No sunlight reaches the garden beneath 20 km of ice | The shielding is physically plausible. The website's light-fade scale is illustrative.
Oxygen | Negligible free dissolved oxygen | Explicit model assumption. Lack of photosynthesis does NOT imply oxygen is impossible: non-biological production and transport are possible.
Food from above | No photosynthesis-derived food rains down from the ice | Assumption. The energy pathway considered here is local chemical production, not an assertion that all ocean water must be sterile.

4b. HYDROGEN-POWERED PRIMARY PRODUCTION

As water interacts with olivine-rich rock, serpentinization can release molecular hydrogen. At Earth's Lost City hydrothermal field, this type of reaction supplies reduced chemicals (Kelley et al., 2005). Cassini measured molecular hydrogen in Enceladus' plume, supporting the possibility of ongoing water-rock reactions and chemical energy there (Waite et al., 2017). Earth methanogens can use the following reaction under appropriate conditions:

CO₂ + 4H₂ → CH₄ + 2H₂O

The reaction releases usable chemical energy; organisms use it to grow. Taubner et al. (2018) demonstrated biological methane production under some simulated Enceladus-like laboratory conditions. That does not demonstrate growth under every combination of our proposed temperature, pH, pressure and nutrient supply.

A central uncertainty is usable carbon. In very alkaline water, dissolved inorganic carbon tends to occur mainly as bicarbonate and carbonate rather than freely dissolved CO₂. We assume circulation transports enough biologically accessible inorganic carbon into the warm chimney pores. Pyrothalasa's carbon concentration and uptake pathways are not measured; high hydrogen alone cannot ensure microbial growth. The abiotic carbon chemistry at Lost City also differs from the simple picture of seawater CO₂ entering the vents (Proskurowski et al., 2008).

Tidal heating could help water circulate through reactive rock for an extended period, as explored in models of Enceladus (Choblet et al., 2017). The duration and rate on our fictional moon are assumptions, not measured values.

4c. MAJOR DESIGN DECISIONS AND EVIDENCE

Decision 1 — Hydrogen from water-rock reactions.
Evidence: Lost City has hydrogen-rich hydrothermal chemistry; Cassini found hydrogen in Enceladus' plume (Kelley et al., 2005; Waite et al., 2017).
Assumption: Pyrothalasa maintains an adequate supply of reactive rock, fluid circulation and sustained hydrogen flux.

Decision 2 — Methanogens in warm chimney pores.
Evidence: Methane-related microbial communities occur at Lost City; biological methane formation was tested under some Enceladus-like conditions (Kelley et al., 2005; Taubner et al., 2018).
Assumption: Similar organisms exist on Pyrothalasa, with enough hydrogen and usable inorganic carbon at suitable pH and temperature. Habitable does not mean inhabited.

Decision 3 — One dark, salty subsurface vent field.
Evidence: Measurements and modelling support Enceladus' global ocean, plume salts, and hydrothermal indications (Thomas et al., 2016; Postberg et al., 2009; Hsu et al., 2015).
Assumption: Our fictional moon has comparable physical properties, and the chosen garden is representative of one active vent field.

Decision 4 — A short, microscopic, anaerobic food web.
Evidence: Anaerobic protists can consume certain bacteria (Hirakata et al., 2020); a report of permanently anaerobic Loricifera exists but its interpretation has been challenged (Danovaro et al., 2010; Bernhard et al., 2015; Danovaro et al., 2016).
Assumption: A viable microbial food community, specialized grazers and Shadowfin could function together without oxygen. Neither the complete food chain nor the predator has been demonstrated.

4d. ENERGY FLOW AND FOOD WEB

SOURCE: Rock + water → hydrogen (H₂)
LEVEL 1 — METHANE-MAKERS: Methanogens use H₂ and available inorganic carbon to gain energy and build biomass.
LEVEL 2 — GRAZERS: Fictional anaerobic single-celled consumers. We hypothesize that they eat suitable bacteria supported by organic matter made and recycled within the local microbial community. They are NOT proven to survive on methanogenic archaea alone. Hirakata et al. (2020) found that some test methanogenic archaea did not support anaerobic protist growth.
LEVEL 3 — SHADOWFIN: Fictional, eyeless, shell-bearing microscopic predator under 1 mm, proposed to feed on grazers. Its entire anaerobic life cycle and energy budget are hypothetical. The Loricifera literature is debated and provides inspiration, not proof.

Energy is not fully transferred to the next feeding level: some is used in metabolism or dissipated as heat, and some remains in uneaten material and waste. The 1,000 → 100 → 10 'energy coin' illustration assumes 10% transfer at each step. These are NOT measured values or a prediction for Pyrothalasa. The actual energy fluxes and whether they support a predator are unknown. If hydrogen becomes limiting, primary production would decrease and downstream consumers could lose food; the rate and order of any population collapse are speculative.

REJECTED ALTERNATIVE

We considered an Earth-style black-smoker food web with large tubeworms and sulfide-oxidizing symbionts. Many such Earth pathways require oxygen supplied ultimately by surface photosynthesis. We rejected reliance on a substantial oxygen supply for our chosen habitat and instead assumed negligible dissolved oxygen, using hydrogen-powered methanogenesis as the base. Darkness alone does not prove oxygen is absent, and oxygen-independent sulfur metabolisms can also exist; our choice is a deliberate, narrowly defined design decision rather than proof that all sulfide-based ecosystems are impossible.

6. FEASIBILITY, LIMITATIONS AND VALIDATION

What is plausible: serpentinization can generate H₂, and some Earth methanogens can produce methane without sunlight or oxygen. What is NOT established: sufficient carbon supply, a self-sustaining microbial biomass budget, grazer nutrition, and Shadowfin's oxygen-free predation and reproduction.

Limitations:
(1) Chemical supply: high pH may limit free CO₂; the availability, replenishment and biological use of inorganic carbon are unknown.
(2) Feeding: a grazer cannot automatically grow on methanogens. We assume a suitable secondary bacterial community, but its organic-carbon and energy sources require verification (Hirakata et al., 2020).
(3) Predator: claims of permanently anaerobic Loricifera have been disputed. Shadowfin is fictional, and no evidence establishes the complete life cycle or adequate prey energy budget (Danovaro et al., 2010; Bernhard et al., 2015; Danovaro et al., 2016).
(4) Model simplification: oxygen scarcity, 8 °C garden water, constant gravity and a 10% trophic efficiency are assumptions or teaching approximations.

Validation plan (proposed; no tests claimed completed):
TEST A — Can the base grow? Who: a qualified microbiology lab. How: compare a suitable Earth methanogen under controlled combinations representing the garden's pressure, pH, temperature, gas mixture and plausible dissolved inorganic carbon range. Measure biomass and methane production, with non-biological and low-substrate controls. Success: reproducible biomass growth coupled with methane production at the proposed available carbon and hydrogen levels. This validates only Level 1.
TEST B — Can grazers obtain food? Who: microbial ecology researchers. How: compare anaerobic protists offered methanogen biomass alone, a justified mixed bacterial community, and controls. Measure ingestion, sustained population growth and reproduction. Success: sustained growth on food that the proposed garden could actually supply. This tests Level 2, not Shadowfin.
TEST C — Could a predator be supported? Who: ecosystem modellers. How: estimate hydrogen flux, microbial growth yield, bacterial recycling, grazer production and consumer energy needs. Success: a transparent, positive energy budget across plausible parameter ranges. A model cannot prove Shadowfin exists or evolved.

Optional observational test: a spacecraft could measure H₂, methane and carbon-bearing species in an Enceladus-like plume. These can constrain chemistry, but methane alone is not proof of life.
