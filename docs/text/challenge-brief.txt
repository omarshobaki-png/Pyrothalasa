A  CHALLENGE A · ASTROBIOLOGY AND OCEAN WORLDS

   Life Without Sunlight: Design a Chemosynthetic Ocean
   World

Original NASA Space Beyond Sunlight: An Aquatic Chemosynthetic World

Apps challenge          2024 NASA International Space Apps Challenge (held 5–6 October 2024)

Official sources        2024 challenge listing:
                        https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/nasa-space-apps-2024/challenges/

                        Space Apps announcement of the 2024 challenges:
                        https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/blog/ignite-your-ideas-with-a-challenge

How we adapted it       The original challenge invites teams to design an ocean world, and its ecosystem, where
                        chemosynthesis rather than photosynthesis sustains life. Our version narrows this to one
                        habitat zone, one energy pathway and a small food web, and asks teams to justify each
                        design choice with cited science. No coding or simulation is needed.

BACKGROUND AND REAL-WORLD RELEVANCE
Most life we know depends on sunlight. In Earth’s deep ocean, however, communities around hydrothermal vents
and cold seeps are powered by chemosynthesis: microbes use chemical energy, such as from hydrogen sulfide
or methane, to build the base of a food web.

Ocean worlds beyond Earth make this idea more than a curiosity. NASA’s Cassini mission found evidence of
hydrogen entering the ocean of Saturn’s moon Enceladus through hydrothermal activity, a possible chemical
energy source for life. “Habitable” does not mean “inhabited”: this challenge is about reasoning carefully about
what could be possible.

PROBLEM STATEMENT

   Students and the public find it hard to imagine how an ecosystem could run without sunlight. Design a
   fictional but scientifically reasoned ocean world, focused on one habitat zone, in which
   chemosynthesis is the base of the food web, and explain it so a non-specialist can follow your
   reasoning.

YOUR TASK

 1.​ Define your habitat zone: where it is in your ocean world and its key conditions (for example depth or
      pressure, temperature range, main chemicals).

 2.​ Identify the energy source and show how energy passes from it through microbes to at least two further levels
      of life.

 3.​ Justify each major design choice with real science or observations, and say what is assumption rather than
      evidence.

 4.​ Explain the limits of your idea and how scientists could test whether such a zone is plausible.

INTENDED USERS OR BENEFICIARIES
Students and members of the public learning about astrobiology, and the educators or science communicators
who would use your concept to teach it. Scientists exploring where life might exist beyond Earth are a secondary
audience.

FIVE GUIDING QUESTIONS

 1.​ What process supplies energy in your world, and why would that supply last long enough for an ecosystem to
      depend on it?

 2.​ Which chemical reactions could microbes use, and where on Earth or in our solar system is there evidence of
      something similar?

 3.​ How do conditions such as pressure, temperature, salinity and darkness shape what organisms need in order
      to survive?

 4.​ How does energy move from its source to the largest organism in your ecosystem, and what is lost along the
      way?

5.​ What would happen if the energy supply stopped, and what observation could show that your world is
    plausible?

MINIMUM SOLUTION REQUIREMENTS

 •​ One named habitat zone with at least four stated conditions, including the energy source.
 •​ An energy-flow diagram with at least three levels of life.
 •​ At least three design decisions, each linked to a cited real-world fact.
 •​ At least two stated limitations and a simple validation plan.
 •​ At least four cited sources, two of them from NASA, NOAA or another space or science agency, or

     peer-reviewed.

SCOPE AND CONSTRAINTS

 •​ In scope: fictional organisms and worlds that obey physics and chemistry you can cite; one zone, not a whole
     planet.

 •​ Out of scope: claiming that life exists on a real world; unexplained or “magic” energy sources; detailed

B  CHALLENGE B · EARTH OBSERVATION AND AGRICULTURE

   Water Wise: A Satellite-Informed Decision Concept for
   Farmers

BACKGROUND AND REAL-WORLD RELEVANCE
Farmers face water-related risks such as drought, flooding, or both in the same season. These affect crop health,
income and food security. NASA satellites observe conditions that matter to these decisions, including rainfall
(GPM), soil moisture (SMAP) and groundwater change (GRACE-FO).
The data are free to use, but turning satellite observations into guidance a farmer can act on is difficult. Where the
data come from, how current they are and how detailed they are all affect whether they are useful. This challenge
is about making that connection clearly and honestly.

PROBLEM STATEMENT

   Farmers make time-sensitive water decisions with limited information, and free satellite data are rarely
   presented in a form they can use. Choose one farmer and one water-related decision, then design a
   concept that turns relevant NASA Earth observations into clear, actionable guidance, and explain its
   limits.

YOUR TASK

 1.​ Define your farmer (who, where, what type of farm) and the one water-related decision they face.
 2.​ Identify which kinds of satellite observation could inform that decision, and what each one can and cannot tell

      the farmer.
 3.​ Design how the guidance reaches the farmer and what they would do with it.
 4.​ Explain the limits of your idea and how you would test it with farmers or local information.

INTENDED USERS OR BENEFICIARIES
Farmers of the type you choose, such as smallholder or commercial growers, and the people who support them,
such as agricultural advisers or cooperatives. Describe your users specifically and explain why you chose them.

FIVE GUIDING QUESTIONS

 1.​ Who exactly is your farmer, what water-related decision do they face and when, and what does a wrong
      decision cost?

 2.​ What information does the farmer already use, and what is missing?
 3.​ Which kinds of NASA observations are relevant to this decision, and how often are they updated and how

      detailed are they?
 4.​ How will the guidance reach the farmer, considering device, language, connectivity and trust, and in what

      form?
 5.​ How could you check that the guidance is correct and useful before building anything large?

MINIMUM SOLUTION REQUIREMENTS

 •​ A farmer profile, region and one clearly stated decision.
 •​ A data-to-decision map linking at least two observation types to the decision, noting update frequency and

     detail from a cited source.
 •​ One real NASA observation example (a screenshot or cited figure with date, place and what it shows).
 •​ A user-flow visual such as a wireframe or storyboard.
 •​ At least two stated limitations and a simple validation plan.
 •​ At least four cited sources, two of them from NASA or another space agency, or peer-reviewed.

SCOPE AND CONSTRAINTS

 •​ In scope: one decision for one farmer type; browsing existing NASA imagery or reading NASA pages for your
     example.

 •​ Out of scope: downloading or processing datasets; building a working app; claiming accuracy you have not
     tested; professional agronomic advice; any suggestion that NASA endorses your concept.

SECTION F

Evaluation rubric

One common rubric, 100 points, is used for both challenges. Each submission is scored independently by
two judges and the scores are averaged. We do not give points simply for using AI, writing code, building
expensive prototypes or producing visually elaborate presentations.

Problem understanding and relevance 20 points

Excellent   17–20  The problem and users are precisely defined and narrowed to a manageable
                   focus. The real-world or scientific relevance is clear and the challenge’s intent is
                   faithfully followed.

Strong      12–16 The problem and users are clear with minor vagueness. Relevance is explained
                         and the focus is mostly appropriate.

Developing  6–11   The problem is broad or generic. Users or relevance are only partly explained, or
                   the work drifts from the challenge.

Limited     0–5 The problem is unclear or off-topic, only restates the challenge, or users are not
                        identified.

Scientific reasoning and use of evidence 20 points

Excellent   17–20  Key claims are explained with correct science or data. Sources are accurate, meet
                   the minimum and are tied to specific decisions. Evidence and assumptions are
                   clearly separated.

Strong      12–16 Reasoning is mostly correct and sources are linked to most decisions. There are
                         minor gaps or unflagged assumptions.

Developing  6–11   Some correct facts appear but are weakly linked to the design. Sources are listed
                   but little used.

Limited     0–5 Claims are unsupported or incorrect, or sources are missing or cannot be verified.

Creativity and justification of the approach 20 points

Excellent   17–20 The approach is original or a thoughtful combination of ideas. The team explains
                         why it chose this approach and compares at least one alternative.

Strong      12–16 There is a fresh element and the choice is reasonably explained.

Developing  6–11 The idea is familiar and there is little justification for choosing it.

Limited     0–5 The idea is generic or copied, with no justification.

Totals and ties

Total = 20 + 20 + 20 + 20 + 10 + 10 = 100 points. The 15 highest-scoring eligible teams across both
challenges are selected; there is no quota for either challenge. Ties are broken by higher Scientific reasoning,
then Problem understanding, then Feasibility, then an independent review.
