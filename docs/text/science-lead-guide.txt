Ready-to-use paragraph (about 90 words):

The Hydrogen Garden is a single field of white chimneys on the seafloor of
Pyrothalasa, our fictional icy moon. It lies 50 km down, under 20 km of ice and 30 km
of salty ocean, where the pressure is about 56 bar. No sunlight reaches it, so there is
no photosynthesis and no oxygen. Warm, very alkaline fluid rich in hydrogen seeps
from the chimneys into water that is otherwise near freezing. We modelled the garden
on Lost City, a real vent field in the Atlantic, and the ocean on Saturn's moon
Enceladus.

4b · The energy source

As seawater soaks through rock rich in olivine, the two react (serpentinisation) and
release hydrogen gas. Methane-making microbes then combine that hydrogen with
carbon dioxide and use the energy released to grow:
CO₂ + 4H₂ → CH₄ + 2H₂O
Write the equation as plain text like this. A formula block shows up as raw code in the PDF
export.

     The strongest evidence, use it: Cassini's hydrogen measurement shows that
     Enceladus' ocean chemistry favours making methane from CO₂ (Waite 2017). That
     links our microbes to Enceladus itself, not only to Lost City. It answers guiding
     question 2.
     Where else we've seen it: at Lost City, seawater reacting with mantle rock makes
     hydrogen- and methane-rich fluid, and methane-cycling microbes live inside the warm
     chimneys (Kelley 2005). An Earth methane-maker also grew in the lab under
     Enceladus-like conditions (Taubner 2018).
     Why it lasts: tidal heating from Saturn keeps the rocky core warm, and models
     suggest hydrothermal activity could continue for tens of millions to billions of years
     (Choblet 2017, still to check). It answers guiding question 1.
     Uncertainty to name: at Lost City the carbon comes from the rock, not from seawater
     CO₂ (Proskurowski 2008), so we assume enough CO₂ reaches the chimney pores.
Ready-to-use paragraph (about 110 words):

   Our garden runs on hydrogen. When seawater soaks into rock rich in the mineral
   olivine, the two react and release hydrogen gas, a process called serpentinisation.
   Microbes called methanogens combine that hydrogen with carbon dioxide to make
   methane, and they use the energy released to grow (CO₂ + 4H₂ → CH₄ + 2H₂O). This
   needs no sunlight and no oxygen. It happens today at Lost City on Earth, and Cassini
   found hydrogen in the plume of Saturn's moon Enceladus, at levels that favour exactly
   this reaction. Heat from Saturn's tides could keep the rock reacting for millions of
   years, long enough for an ecosystem to depend on it.

Ready-to-use paragraph (about 100 words):

We first considered copying Earth's famous black-smoker vents, where giant
tubeworms live on bacteria that use hydrogen sulfide. But that reaction needs oxygen,
and the oxygen in Earth's deep sea is made by photosynthesis at the sunlit surface, so
even these "sunless" vents depend on the Sun. We then considered letting a little
oxygen seep down from the surface, as may happen on Europa, so that larger animals
could breathe. We rejected that too: it is uncertain, and it would weaken our first
argument. Instead our whole food web runs without oxygen, so its life stays
microscopic.
