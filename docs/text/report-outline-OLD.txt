 How to use this doc

  This is the plan for our 4-page report (the main deliverable). Every number or claim in the
  report, site and video must come from the Master fact table at the bottom.

    1. No table row, no claim. If a fact is not in the table, it does not go in the report, the site
       or the video.

    2. Two-person rule. Anyone can add a row. The Evidence & Critic (Person 3) checks it
       against the source and fills in "Checked by".

    3. Tag everything. Each row is either Evidence (a real source says it) or Assumption (our
       choice). Fictional organisms are marked Fiction.

    4. Log every change. When we change a decision, add one line to the decision log on
       page 4: what, why, who.

    5. Plain words. Write for a high schooler. Define every science word the first time it
       appears.

  Deadline check: the brief says "submit by 10 Nov, 14:00" on pages 2 and 4, but page 3 says
  the 24-hour window ends "9 Nov 14:00". Email the organizer today to confirm which one is
  right.
  Rules from the brief: at most 4 pages, text no smaller than 10 pt, including visuals and
  references. The file is named TEAMNAME_Challenge-A_Report.pdf with no spaces in the
  team name.

Page 1 — Who we are, the problem, our idea

Section 1 · Team identification (P5, 2 lines). Team name, Challenge A: Life Without
Sunlight, and all 6 member names with their roles.

Section 2 · Problem and intended users (P4, about 120 words).

     Problem: high-school students find it hard to imagine a food web with no sunlight at
     the bottom of it. Every food chain they learn starts with a plant.
     Users: students aged 14–18 and the science teachers who teach them. Scientists are
     not our audience.
     Narrow focus: one habitat zone (a vent field), one energy pathway (hydrogen), and a
     4-level food web.
     Starter sentence: "Every food chain in a school textbook starts with sunlight. We set
     out to show a high schooler, in under five minutes, how a whole ecosystem could run
     without it."

Section 3 · Proposed solution and how it works (P2 and P6, about 200 words).

     The idea: the Hydrogen Garden, a fictional vent field 50 km down in the ocean of an
     icy moon we invented, modelled on Saturn's moon Enceladus.
     How the parts fit: (1) the report explains the science, (2) the interactive dive lets a
     student scroll from the ice surface down to the vents and tap each label for its fact
     and source, and (3) the video walks through it in 3 minutes.
     Key sentence: "Rock reacts with water and releases hydrogen. Microbes turn that
     hydrogen into food. Three levels of animals live off those microbes."
     P6 adds 2 lines on the creature mode: at each level the student plays that creature
     and follows where its energy goes.
     Put the site link + QR code here.

Section 5 · Main visual (P5, half the page). A cross-section from the ice down to the
vents, with energy-flow arrows: hydrogen → methane-makers → scrapers / fan-feeders →
Shadowfin, with the heat lost at each step. Label at least 4 conditions directly on the
drawing (depth, pressure, temperature, chemicals, darkness). Caption: "Energy flow in the
Hydrogen Garden. Green = evidence, amber = our assumption."

4b · The energy source (about 150 words). Seawater reacts with rock rich in olivine
(serpentinisation) and releases hydrogen gas. Methane-making microbes use it:

  CO_2 + 4H_2 \rightarrow CH_4 + 2H_2O

     Why it lasts: tidal heating from Saturn keeps the rocky core warm. Models suggest
     hydrothermal activity could last tens of millions to billions of years (Choblet 2017).
     This answers guiding question 1.
     Where we've seen it: Lost City on Earth (Kelley 2005), and hydrogen in Enceladus'
     plume (Waite 2017). This answers guiding question 2.

Guiding question 3 (how conditions shape organisms): one sentence each. No light
means no eyes; low energy means slow, small animals; microbes are fine at vent
temperatures, since one methane-maker grows at up to 122 °C (Takai 2008).

Page 3 — Food web, the rejected alternative, limits and testing

4d · Food web and energy loss (P2, about 150 words + a small diagram by P5).

  1. Methane-makers (Level 1): microbe mats on the chimneys that turn H₂ + CO₂ into
     food.

  2. Scrapers (Level 2): snail-like grazers that scrape off the mats. Fiction, based on the
     snails and limpets at Earth's vents (NOAA poster).

  3. Fan-feeders (Level 3): catch drifting microbes, scraps and scraper waste. Fiction,
     based on filter feeders at cold seeps (NOAA poster).

  4. Shadowfin (Level 4): an eyeless, hand-sized predator. Fiction.
     Energy loss: start with 1,000 "energy coins" at Level 1. At ~10% per step (Pauly &
     Christensen 1995) that leaves 100, then 10, then 1 coin for Shadowfin. Say clearly that
     the coins are illustrative. This answers guiding question 4.
     If the vent stops: the mats starve first, then the grazers and filter feeders, then
     Shadowfin. Life continues only where new vents open (NOAA: vents are short-lived).
     This answers guiding question 5.

Creativity · The alternative we rejected (P1, about 100 words). This is worth up to 20
points, so don't skip it.

     Alternative: copy Earth's famous black-smoker vents, with giant tubeworms living on
     hydrogen-sulfide-eating bacteria.
     Why we rejected it: NOAA writes that reaction as CO₂ + 4H₂S + O₂ → CH₂O + 4S +
     3H₂O. It needs oxygen, and Earth's deep-sea oxygen comes from photosynthesis at
     the sunlit surface. A moon with no sunlight has no such oxygen supply. Hydrogen +
     CO₂ needs no oxygen, and both were found in Enceladus' plume (Waite 2017).
     Starter sentence: "Earth's 'sunless' vent ecosystems secretly still depend on the Sun.
     Ours does not."
     Optional second comparison (1 line, our reasoning only): why a scroll-down dive
     instead of a static poster. Scrolling makes 50 km of depth feel real.

Section 6 · Feasibility, limitations and validation (P3, about 200 words).

What is realistic: every chemical step has been observed on Earth or measured at
Enceladus. Whether life actually exists there is unknown: habitable does not mean
inhabited.

Limitations (the brief asks for at least 2):

     Our organisms are invented from Earth examples, and real alien life could be very
     different.
     The ~10% rule is an Earth average that varies a lot. Making methane also yields less
     energy than breathing oxygen, so the real food web could be even smaller.
     Our gauges use simplified physics: constant gravity, a straight-line temperature
     change through the ice, and a made-up light fade rate.
     Our depth, temperature and chimney numbers are borrowed from Enceladus and Lost
     City. They are not measurements of our fictional moon.
